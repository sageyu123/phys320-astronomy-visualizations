What is a shearing patch?
We follow a small box that rides around the Sun at the local orbital speed. Inside the box, bodies closer to the Sun orbit a little faster and drift ahead; bodies farther out drift behind: that sliding is the shear: a body a distance outside the box centre drifts along the orbit at relative to the box, where is the orbital angular speed. Every disk around a star has it, because orbital speed falls with distance (Kepler’s third law); only a disk rotating like a solid wheel would not, and no orbiting disk does. Orbits shear because angular speed falls with distance, (Kepler’s third law); a disk spinning like a solid wheel would not shear. The shearing brings neighbours slowly past a growing body, which sweeps up those within a few Hill radii; once that strip is empty, growth stops at the isolation mass. The box lets us watch 300 bodies in a local region; its physical width depends on this model’s starting-body scale (shown in the inset). Its edges wrap around, so whatever leaves one side re-enters on the other.
Why Jupiter matters
Jupiter’s tugs excite belt orbits to e ≈ 0.05–0.15, giving typical encounter speeds of about 1–2 km/s at 2.8 AU. That is 5–15 times a 100-km body’s ≈0.1 km/s escape speed, so collisions can grind bodies into dust while the matched patch without Jupiter grows.
